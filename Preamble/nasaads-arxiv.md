# Finding and following the research literature

Research is not only about reading one paper. It is about learning how papers are connected to each other.

A single paper can lead you backward to the work it cites, forward to newer papers that cite it, sideways to related papers, and outward to data, software, and follow-up studies. Learning how to follow these connections is one of the most important skills in research.

Two especially useful tools for astronomy and astrophysics are:

- [NASA ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/)
- [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/)

NASA ADS is usually the best place to search the astronomy literature and follow citation networks. arXiv is useful for finding recent preprints, including papers that may not yet be published in a peer-reviewed journal.

## NASA ADS

[NASA ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/) is one of the most important literature-search tools in astronomy and astrophysics. It is more than a search engine. It is a way to explore the web of papers connected to a research topic.

### What NASA ADS is useful for

In ADS, you can search by author, title, keyword, abstract text, publication year, or combinations of these. Once you find a paper, ADS usually provides links to the available versions of the article, including the publisher page, published PDF, arXiv preprint, or other full-text sources when available.

ADS is especially useful when you want to:

- find a paper from a title, author, keyword, or citation,
- locate the published version of a paper,
- locate the arXiv version of a paper,
- export citations,
- follow references and citations,
- find related papers,
- build a library of papers,
- create keyword alerts,
- explore how a topic developed over time.

### Starting from a topic

If you are starting with a broad topic, begin with a keyword search. For example, you might search for phrases such as:

```text
circumbinary planets
planet stability binary stars
eclipsing binaries eclipse timing variations
transit timing variations exoplanets
```

A broad search will often return many papers. This is normal. Your first goal is not to read every paper. Your first goal is to identify which papers are central, recent, or especially relevant.

Useful strategies include:

- sort by **date** to find recent work,
- sort by **citation count** to find influential papers,
- sort by **read count** to see papers that have been accessed recently,
- narrow the search using author names, publication years, or more specific keywords,
- save useful papers to a library or research log.

A good search usually becomes more specific as you learn the vocabulary of the field.

### Starting from one paper

Sometimes the best way to begin is with one known paper. This might be a paper your advisor recommended, a paper cited in a textbook, or a paper that appears repeatedly in search results.

Once you open the paper in ADS, look for links to:

- **References** — papers cited by the current paper,
- **Citations** — newer papers that cite the current paper,
- **Co-Reads** — papers that other readers of the current paper also viewed,
- **Full Text Sources** — links to the published article, arXiv version, or available PDFs.

These links help you move through the literature.

References take you backward in time. They show the earlier work that the authors built upon.

Citations take you forward in time. They show how later authors used, tested, corrected, or extended the work.

Co-Reads can help you find nearby papers that may not be directly connected by citation but are still relevant to the same topic.

### Following the paper web

A useful research habit is to build a small paper web around a topic.

Start with one important paper and ask:

1. What are the most important papers it cites?
2. Which newer papers cite it?
3. Are there review articles or introduction-heavy papers that explain the background?
4. Do the same author names appear repeatedly?
5. Do the same equations, models, datasets, or assumptions appear repeatedly?
6. Are there disagreements between papers?
7. Has the result been updated, corrected, or challenged by later work?

This process helps you understand the conversation happening in the literature. A paper is not an isolated object. It is part of a larger scientific discussion.

### Using myADS alerts

If you create an ADS account, you can use **myADS** to receive paper alerts based on keywords or saved searches.

This is useful when you want to keep up with a research topic without manually searching every day. For example, you could create alerts for phrases such as:

```text
circumbinary planets
eclipse timing variations
planet stability binary stars
TESS eclipsing binaries
```

ADS can deliver regular email updates when new papers match your selected keywords or searches. This is a good way to slowly build familiarity with the field.

When you receive an alert, you do not need to read every paper in detail. Instead, scan the titles and abstracts. Save the papers that seem relevant to your project, and write a short note about why they might matter.

## arXiv

[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/) is an open-access archive for research papers in fields such as physics, astronomy, mathematics, computer science, statistics, and related areas.

### What arXiv is for

In astronomy, many researchers post papers to arXiv before, during, or after the journal publication process. This makes arXiv useful for finding very recent work.

arXiv is especially useful when you want to:

- find recent preprints,
- follow new work in a specific field,
- access papers that may be behind journal paywalls,
- read a paper before the final published version appears,
- see updates or revisions to a paper,
- browse new submissions in a subject area.

### Preprints are not the same as published papers

A paper on arXiv is not automatically a peer-reviewed publication. A paper on arXiv may be:

- a preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed,
- a submitted paper that is still under review,
- a revised version of a paper,
- a paper that has already been accepted or published,
- a conference proceeding or other scholarly document.

Because of this, you should not assume that every arXiv paper is correct or final. The results may change before publication. Some papers may contain mistakes. Some may never be published in a peer-reviewed journal.

A good rule is:

> Treat arXiv as an early-access research tool, not as a guarantee of correctness.

### New and recent arXiv submissions

arXiv has pages that list newly submitted and recently updated papers. For astronomy, useful pages include:

- [astro-ph new submissions](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph/new)
- [astro-ph recent submissions](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph/recent)
- [astro-ph.EP: Earth and Planetary Astrophysics](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph.EP/recent)

The **new** page shows the latest submissions for the current posting cycle. The **recent** page is useful if you want to browse papers from the last several posting days.

This can be a good habit once you are more comfortable with the field. At first, the daily list may feel overwhelming. That is normal. You are not expected to understand every title. Instead, practice recognizing keywords, author names, methods, and topics that connect to your project.

### Reading an arXiv entry

When looking at an arXiv paper, pay attention to the metadata near the title and abstract. Useful information may include:

- the submission date,
- whether the paper has been revised,
- comments from the authors,
- journal reference information,
- the subject category,
- links to the PDF or HTML version,
- links to related versions of the paper.

If the arXiv page includes a journal reference, the paper may already be published or accepted. If it says “submitted to” or “comments welcome,” then the paper may not yet have completed peer review.

When in doubt, search for the paper in ADS. ADS can often help you find whether there is a published version, an arXiv version, or both.

## Using ADS and arXiv together

ADS and arXiv are complementary.

Use **arXiv** when you want to see very recent preprints.

Use **ADS** when you want to place a paper in context by finding its published version, references, citations, related papers, and citation metadata.

A common workflow is:

1. Find a new paper on arXiv.
2. Search for the paper in ADS.
3. Save it to an ADS library.
4. Look at its references and citations.
5. Check whether the paper is published, accepted, submitted, or still only a preprint.
6. Add a short summary to your research log.

### arXiv videos

For arXiv, short overview videos can be helpful, but the most important ideas are simple:

- arXiv is useful for finding recent preprints,
- arXiv papers are not automatically peer-reviewed publications,
- a preprint may be revised, accepted, published, or corrected later,
- important claims should be checked against the published literature when available.

The official arXiv documentation is usually more reliable than a random video. Useful starting points include:

- [About arXiv](https://info.arxiv.org/about/index.html)
- [arXiv Help Pages](https://info.arxiv.org/help/index.html)
- [arXiv astro-ph new submissions](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph/new)
- [arXiv astro-ph recent submissions](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph/recent)

If you watch an arXiv overview video, use it only as an introduction. Then open arXiv yourself and practice finding a recent astro-ph paper, checking its submission history, reading the abstract, and searching for the same paper in NASA ADS.

## Trust, but verify

Literature-search tools are powerful, but they do not remove the need for judgment.

When reading any paper, ask:

- Has this work been peer reviewed?
- Is this the preprint version or the published version?
- Are there later papers that corrected or challenged the result?
- Are the assumptions appropriate for my project?
- Are the data, code, or methods available?
- Do other papers reach similar conclusions?
- Is this a central result or a speculative interpretation?

For arXiv papers especially, be careful not to treat every claim as settled science. A preprint can be useful and important, but it should still be checked against later versions, published literature, and your advisor’s guidance.

## Beginner workflow

If you are beginning a new research topic, try this workflow:

1. Search ADS using a few broad keywords.
2. Identify two or three papers that appear central or repeatedly cited.
3. Open one paper and inspect its references.
4. Open the list of papers that cite it.
5. Save useful papers to a library or research log.
6. Search arXiv for recent papers on the same topic.
7. Compare the recent papers to the older foundational papers.
8. Write a short summary of what you found.

Your summary should include:

- the main topic,
- the papers you found,
- why each paper seems relevant,
- what you understand so far,
- what you do not yet understand,
- what you should read next.

The goal is not to master the literature in one sitting. The goal is to build a map of the field one paper at a time.
